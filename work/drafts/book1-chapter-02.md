# Chapter 2

The bell over the door didn't so much ring as clatter, cheap tin on hinges that had outlived it by decades. Warmth came next, then the smell: yeast, something char-edged from the oven, cinnamon underneath both. The ovens had the place running warm enough that he should have shed the coat. He didn't.

Behind the counter a woman was working a tray free of the bottom oven with a towel that had given up being white a long time ago. Shorter than the counter made her look, dirty blond hair mostly pinned up and losing the argument.

"We're open till eight, come on in..." She looked up mid-sentence and stopped. Not at him. Past him, at the door he had just come through, a beat longer than a glance cost anybody. Her brow did something small and quick before it smoothed back out. "Sorry. Come in, sit wherever."

Leo didn't ask what she'd been looking at. He filed it and let the door swing shut behind him.

Barbara had said Agnes. There wasn't anybody else working.

The place was smaller than it read from the street. Three pastries left in the glass case, round loaves cooling on a wire rack, an espresso machine that had seen more polish than the counter it sat on. A chalkboard by the register had the day's specials in handwriting too neat to be anybody's afterthought. The corner table had two cups on it.

"Sourdough's the last one," Agnes said, nodding at it.

"I'll take it. And whatever's left in the case. Coffee too, if you're still doing them."

"Long day?"

"Long year."

She didn't push past that, rang him up instead, and he couldn't tell yet whether that was manners or a habit of not asking questions she didn't want answered back.

He carried the bag and the coffee to a table with a clean line of sight to the counter and spread three folders open in front of him, the top one tabbed MUNROE, G. in his own block capitals. Old habit: never look like you're watching somebody, look like you're reading, and turn a page every so often on a count so the room believes it. It worked fine on interrogation rooms. He was less sure about bakeries.

He watched her over the top of the Munroe file. She checked the oven timer without looking at it. Either she ran the tightest bakery on the street or she was the kind of person who knew things other people would pay to know.

She kept looking at the corner table. A half-second flick every few minutes, covered each time by reaching for a rag or checking the register. He followed the line of it three times before he made himself stop, and by then he had what was actually sitting there: two cups of tea, cold enough that a skin had set on top of both, no chair pulled out, nobody within six feet of them.

Weird woman. He liked her already, and he wasn't sure that reflected well on either of them.

He was halfway through the coffee when he noticed the collar wasn't fighting him for once. He unzipped the coat without deciding to and shrugged it half off his shoulders.

First time since fall. He put it down to the ovens and went back to the file.

He finished the coffee without deciding anything. Out of leads is out of leads, whatever the source. A year ago he'd have laughed at a county clerk sending him to a baker.

He gathered the folders, left the crumbs, and carried the empty cup to the counter instead of the table.

"Can I ask you something."

"You've been working up to it for twenty minutes." She said it the way she'd quote him a price.

"Barbara. County records. Said you help people out sometimes, with things that don't have a normal fix."

"Barbara talks a lot for someone who stamps things all day."

"She's not wrong, though."

Agnes wiped her hands down the apron, slow. "Depends what you're bringing me."

"Something I can't get anyone official to look at twice."

"Come back after we close, then. Eight thirty, door'll still be open." She paused over the register, not quite closing it. "I was about to ask you the same thing, actually."

"Ask me what?"

"To come back." She turned for the ovens. "Save us both the trip."
