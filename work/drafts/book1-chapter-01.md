# Six to Eight Weeks

Barbara stamped the request before she had finished reading it, the same as she had stamped the last three.

"This one's not going through as a counter request." She tapped GEOFF MUNROE where he'd printed it in block letters. "Archive request. Six to eight weeks."

"I used to be able to walk these down myself."

"I know." She didn't look up to say it. Six months of *I used to* from the same man wore a groove in a person eventually.

He signed where she tapped, folded the yellow copy into one of the jacket's several inside pockets, and didn't leave.

She noticed him not leaving. She was the only clerk in the building who had ever bothered to recognize him twice, and he had never worked out why. The coat, probably. He had worn the same one in here every visit since fall, zipped to the throat no matter how hot they kept the place.

"You look like hell." Not unkind. "Eat something. There's a bakery on Lemel, they still got sourdough this time of day if you're quick."

"I'll live."

"Didn't say you wouldn't." She was already reaching for the next form in her stack, but she said the rest of it anyway, like it was worth the extra breath. "Woman who runs the place, Agnes, she's helped some people out. Not baking stuff. Found somebody's cat that'd been gone three weeks. Dug up a property ledger the county lost in a fire twenty years back, don't ask me how. If something's eating at you, she's worth a conversation. Cheaper than what you're doing now, anyway."

"What am I doing now."

"Standing in my line looking like you haven't slept since March." She finally looked up. "Go eat some bread, Leo."

Six accidents on the mountain stretch past exit 43 in fourteen months, all on the same four-mile grade, all ruled the same way: bad road, bad light, a curve nobody clocked fast enough in the dark. He had spent the better part of a year trying to get somebody to explain why a bad stretch of road picked its nights so specifically.

He had pulled three of the six files before anyone told him to stop. He had talked to two of the families before someone made it clear, in the flat tone captains used when they had already decided a conversation was over, that talking to grieving families about a closed case wasn't an active investigation, it was a liability. He kept going anyway, on his own time, into a case that no longer existed. Munroe's mother cried on her own porch for forty minutes and thanked him for it afterward, which was worse than if she'd told him to leave.

Nobody got fired for asking questions. He got fired for asking the same one after being told to stop.

Lemel Street at that hour was mostly shut-up storefronts and the one bar that always did steady business, and the bakery two doors down from it had its lights on, warm against a sky gone the flat grey that passed for evening lately. A hand-lettered sign in the window said the sourdough went out at eight. There was an old-fashioned bell mounted over the door frame, the kind that rang because you pushed through it.

He pulled the coat tighter walking down Lemel, the way he had every day since fall, and didn't think about why.

He stood on the corner longer than the crossing signal allowed for, then went anyway.
