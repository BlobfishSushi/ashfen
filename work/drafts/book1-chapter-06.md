# Twenty-Six Minutes

Dowd Street at eleven forty at night was nothing at all. One car went by while they were standing in the alley and it didn't slow down.

The sidearm was in the glovebox, under the manual. Burglary was burglary. Burglary with a firearm was a different statute and a different arithmetic, and he knew both numbers without having to look either of them up.

Agnes knelt at the service door and Leo stood where he could see the mouth of the alley and counted. He got to forty-four.

Then they stood just inside the doorway for three full seconds, waiting on a panel four rooms away to say something about it.

It didn't.

Inside it was dark enough that he kept a hand on the wall. Their own breathing, a compressor cycling somewhere, and the smell of old carpet and older heating.

"They're on him," Agnes said, at a level that wasn't quite a whisper. "He's at the front doors. He killed the zones before he stood up, they watched him do it."

They moved. Gallery One was a room full of surveying equipment and a canoe. Gallery Two was entirely about the river. There was a short flight of four steps between Two and Three where the old building had changed its mind about a floor level, and Leo noted the steps and thought nothing else about them.

Gallery Three. The harpoon. The chains.

And between them, on a shelf in a wood-and-glass case with a card in a typeface that went out of fashion before Leo was born: a bell.

Fourteen inches, maybe. Black iron gone brown in patches, thick through the wall, no decoration except a band of shallow marks around the crown that could have been writing and could have been damage. It looked like exactly what the card said it was, which was nothing.

Agnes had the case open in nineteen seconds.

Leo put the towel in first, both hands through the mouth of it, and found the clapper, and wrapped it, and wrapped it again, and taped it, and then held still with his hands inside a four-hundred-year-old bell listening to a building for a count of five.

Nothing.

"Wrapped," he said.

"They know." A pause. "They've gone back to the doorway. They're not coming any closer than that."

He got his hands under it and lifted.

It didn't come.

Nothing was holding it. It came up about an inch and then declined, and he felt the refusal go straight up into his lower back, and he set it down flat and reset his feet and understood, holding it, that every single minute of the plan behind him had been built by a man who assumed he could carry this and walk.

"Agnes."

"I know. Hold on." Then she quoted him directly. "'Oh. Oh, no. Oops.'"

"Oops?"

"'That's a militant one.'"

"Meaning what, exactly?"

"'Ours were smaller. Ours were a third of that, nobody in archives ever had to *lift* one, they sat on stands, you just walked up and...'" Agnes stopped. "He's still going. It's not getting more useful."

"Tell him thank you."

He took it in both arms, up against his chest, planted, and it came off the shelf, and the cold of it went through his jacket like the jacket wasn't there.

They had budgeted nine minutes inside a twenty-six minute round. He was now moving at something under half the speed the plan assumed, and there was no version of the next part where he moved faster, because the thing in his arms had both his hands and wouldn't let him bend, and a man with no hands cannot open a door or catch himself.

The four steps between Gallery Three and Gallery Two took him longer than the entire walk in.

"They're at the far end," Agnes said, from further ahead of him than either of them wanted, because the ghosts wouldn't come near the doorway with it in his arms, and she was the only one who could hear them, and so the whole nervous system of the operation was now a baker in a dark room straining to catch two voices from sixty feet away. "He's finishing the east side. Sable says he's at the coat rack, which puts him turning back this way in about a minute."

"About?"

"She's guessing off eight nights and one coat rack. It's a good guess. Leo, move."

Halfway through Gallery Two the tape shifted. He felt it more than heard it, a fractional change in the balance of the weight, and he stopped dead in the middle of a room full of the river's history.

"What. *What?*"

"Tape."

"Put it *down*, then."

"If I put it down I'm not getting it back up."

She was already there, both hands inside, re-seating the towel by feel with her face turned away from him toward the far doorway, listening to something he had no way of hearing. He could hear her counting under her breath and he didn't think she knew she was doing it.

"Done. Go."

It cost them eleven seconds. He counted every one.

They came out through the service door at twelve oh one, five minutes before Denny wrote down the time he finished. Agnes put the deadbolt back the way she found it, on her knees in the alley for another forty seconds while Leo stood over her holding a piece of iron and not permitted, physically, to help her with anything.

There was no damage anywhere. That was the part he kept coming back to later: no broken pane, not a mark on the case. Whoever eventually stood in Gallery Three with a clipboard was going to be looking at a room that was never entered.

---

The bakery at half past midnight, front lights off, one on in the back.

He set it down on a folded towel on the table nearest the corner, and the table took it badly.

Leo stood over it with his arms shaking in the loose, delayed way arms do, and looked at four hundred years of iron sitting between the napkin holder and the sugar.

"Do you want to sit down?"

"No."

He didn't take his eyes off it.

"One more thing. You two are going to teach me how to ring that."

Agnes waited, listening. "Yes."

"In a room neither of you can be in."

A pause that wasn't hers.

"They're working on it. Aldric says please stop being right about things, it's one in the morning."
