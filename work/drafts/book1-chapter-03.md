# Eight Thirty

The chairs were up on every table but one by the time he got back, legs in the air, waiting on a mop that hadn't happened yet. CLOSED hung in the window in the same hand-lettering as everything else in the place. The door wasn't locked.

Agnes was behind the counter, apron gone, coffee already going. Two mugs this time instead of one.

"Sit," she said, before he'd picked a chair. He took the one facing the door, out of habit, and she didn't comment on it.

He laid the case out the way he had laid it out for eleven months to anyone who'd sit still long enough. Six wrecks, same stretch past exit 43, every file closed the same way. Every driver had passed through a town called Mullerfield in the weeks before it happened, though it had taken him most of a year, one property record and one gas receipt at a time, to find that out. The last one was a kid. Twenty-one, driving home from the state fair.

"That's what I've got," he said. "A road, a town, and no way to explain what connects them that anybody official will listen to."

Agnes didn't write any of it down or ask him to repeat a word. She watched him with the kind of attention he was used to giving people and not to getting.

When he was done, she said, "Before I start, I need you to let me finish. Can you do that?"

"Depends what it is."

"That's not a yes."

"It's the best you're getting tonight."

She took a breath like she was about to lift something heavy. "What I'm about to tell you is going to sound insane. It's also true, every word. I'd rather just say it than spend five minutes arguing with you about whether I've lost it."

"Alright."

"His name's Geoff Munroe. Driving home from the fair, same as you said. Died on the stretch past exit 43." A pause. "There were toadstools growing at the base of the tree he hit. Small ones, the kind that come up after rain."

Leo said nothing for a second. "How do you know his last name. I never gave you his last name."

"I know."

"How do you know about mushrooms at a crash site from fourteen months ago that never made it into any report I've read."

"Because he told me. Today."

"He's dead."

"I know that too." She said it steady.

"That's not proof. That's just you telling me something." It came out harder than he meant it to. "I've been the guy with nothing but his own word for eleven months. I know exactly how far that gets you."

Something in the empty half of the room pulled her attention sideways. She turned her head, not toward Leo, toward nothing he could see, and said, quiet: "Are you sure?"

A pause that wasn't hers to fill.

"Alright."

The air by the corner table went cold enough that he felt it from six feet away, and then there were two people standing in it who hadn't been there a second before. Not solid. Two silhouettes cut from the dim light and filled back in with something that wouldn't sit still. A woman, dark hair pulled back off a face gone hollow under the eyes, pupils blown so wide there was barely any color left around them. A man beside her, curling hair, the same wrong stillness. At the wrong angle Leo could see the edge of the pastry case straight through both of them. Robes the color of wet stone, a cut he'd never seen outside a museum placard.

"We can't hold this long," the man said, his voice arriving a half-second behind his mouth moving. "Agnes does the talking from here. But yes. All of it's true. And yes, this is a lot to be handed on a Tuesday."

The woman didn't say much. She watched him, calm with a stillness too old for the face carrying it. "Sable," she said finally, one word, like a formality she'd decided he was owed.

"Aldric." The man again, and something that might have been the start of a smile crossed whatever was left of his face. "Nice to meet you. Genuinely, kudos for not screaming yet."

Then they were gone, the cold going with them, and the room was a room again.

Leo's hands, he noticed distantly, were flat on the table, pressing down. He was, on the inside, screaming. None of it reached his face.

"They're still here," Agnes said. "That's just what it costs them to be seen by somebody who isn't already looking for it. Not long, not often. I do the talking most days."

"Okay."
