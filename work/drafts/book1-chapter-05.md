# Chapter 5

He was back on Lemel at ten the next morning with a legal pad. The CLOSED sign was still in the window. Agnes let him in without asking why he was early.

"Coffee's not on yet."

"I'll live."

"Did you sleep?"

"Some."

"How much is some?"

"Four hours."

"That's not some, that's a nap." She started the coffee anyway.

He took the same table and wrote three things at the top of the pad, the way he used to put them on a board where other people could see them. *Which building. What's in it. Who's in it at night.* Then he looked up at a room containing one baker and, somewhere in it, two people he couldn't see.

"First one," he said. "Which museum?"

Agnes turned her head toward the corner. Waited through something. Came back.

"They don't know."

"Come again?"

"They know it's in a museum. They don't know which one."

"Then how do they know it's in a museum at all?"

"A woman came through here about five years ago." No apology in it. "Dead about a week. Very upset, and she kept going back to the job, the way people do when they're looking for the good part. She listed off things she'd liked. One of them was a piece of iron nobody could identify, with a card next to it that said something close to Unknown Religious Object." A pause. "Sable knew what it was off three sentences."

"And where did she work?"

"She said 'the museum.' People do that."

"People do that." Leo wrote it down, because writing it down was what he did with everything, including the parts that weren't worth the ink. "So my source is a dead woman, five years ago, describing a paperweight she was fond of."

"You asked for the honest version."

"I'm not complaining, I'm just saying it out loud so I can hear how it sounds." He underlined it twice. "Fine. That half's mine."

It took him nine days.

Nothing about it was interesting. It was accession lists and a state collections index that hadn't been updated since the county paid for it, and one phone call where he said he was writing on regional ecclesiastical metalwork, a sentence he practiced twice in the car first. Small museums answered their phones. Small museums were, in fact, delighted.

The Halbrook was on Dowd, four blocks off the river, in a building that had been a savings and loan until 1953. Municipal. Endowment mostly spent. Object 61-114, iron, provenance unrecorded, acquired in a lot of eleven items out of an estate sale the year the doors opened. Gallery Three, which he learned from the floor plan was the long room at the back, between a whaling harpoon and a set of surveyor's chains.

---

Sable and Aldric went the night he handed over the address, and came back before dawn, and Agnes translated.

It took an hour and a half, and Leo filled six pages.

"One guard," she said. "His name's on the log. Denny."

"Denny."

"That's what they read." She listened. "He comes on at eight. One full round at eleven forty, another at four, and in between he's at the desk in the front office with the door open."

"How long's the round?"

"Twenty-six minutes. Same way every night they've watched. He writes the time in a book when he starts and again when he finishes, and the book stays open on the desk." Her head tilted. "Aldric says the route's taped up over the desk. Laminated. He says whoever laminated it did a nice job."

"What about the alarm?"

"Perimeter's on all night, doors and windows. The inside zones he shuts off before he walks and turns back on after." She paused. "They watched him do it four times to be sure. Same order, same panel, and he says the numbers out loud while he punches them."

Leo put the pen down.

"He says them out loud?"

"Apparently he's alone in there all night and he's been doing it eleven years."

"He says the code out loud, in an empty building, every night, for eleven years."

"That's what they're telling me."

He had spent the better part of a year begging a system for the shape of one closed file. He was now being handed a man's alarm code because two dead people had stood at his elbow and listened.

"You stood behind a man and read his notebook?"

Agnes listened. "Aldric says forty minutes. The man ate an egg salad sandwich the entire time."

"Egg salad."

"He was very clear about the egg salad."

They went back eight more nights. He asked for things and they brought them back: the case in Gallery Three was wood and glass with a cam lock in the front rail, the kind of lock that exists to stop a child. There was a camera over the front desk and none in the back galleries, because the back galleries had chains and harpoons in them. The alley door on the Dowd side was wired to the perimeter loop the same as every other door in the building, except that at eight every night Denny propped it open with a milk crate to carry the trash out, and the panel didn't make a sound about it. Leo had them watch that four nights running before he'd believe it. A dead contact on a service door was the kind of thing that got written on a list and stayed there, in a building that had stopped being able to afford its own maintenance a long time ago.

So the deadbolt was the only real thing between the four of them and a piece of iron nobody had correctly identified in sixty-five years.

---

"Nobody gets caught going in," Leo said.

He had the floor plan out and the pad next to it, and he had been talking for twenty minutes to a table with one person at it.

"They get caught coming out, and they get caught after. In means one door for ninety seconds. After means everything you touched, everything you carried, anybody you told, and the fact that a guy who used to be a cop was pulling accession records on a piece of iron nine days before it walked out of a building." He tapped the pad. "That last one I can't fix. I can only make sure it never gets a reason to be interesting."

"So don't make it interesting."

"So we don't break anything. We don't take anything else. Nobody sees a face. We leave that building the way we found it, Denny writes twenty-six minutes in his book, and in the morning there's a gap on a shelf in a room nobody's walked through since spring." He sat back. "Six weeks from now somebody notices. Six months from now it's a line in an insurance letter."

Agnes turned her cup a half turn on the table. "And the door?"

"And the door." He looked at the plan again, at the little rectangle marked SERV. "That's the problem. That's a real deadbolt on a real frame. I know six people who could open it and I can't call any of them, because a phone call is a person, and a person is what hangs you eventually."

"I can do it."

He looked up.

She was already getting off the stool, and she said it the way she said the sourdough goes out at eight.

"You can do it."

"That's what I said."

The bakery's back door had a deadbolt on it too, older than the one on Dowd. Agnes took a flat leather case out of the apron pocket where she kept her order slips, unfolded it on the step, and picked two pieces out of it without looking. Then she knelt down on a step she had presumably knelt on a thousand times for other reasons and got to work.

Leo timed it out of habit. Thirty-eight seconds.

He wasn't watching the lock. He was watching her hands, and none of what her hands were doing fit in the file he had been keeping on this woman since the first afternoon he sat down in here. She raked it first, fast and sloppy, the way you do when you're finding out what kind of lock you have. Then she stopped raking and went pin by pin, patient, no wasted motion, thumb riding the tension wrench in a spot where the metal had gone bright from years of exactly that. The leather case was soft at the fold. It had been opened and closed a great many times.

She didn't look at the lock once. She looked up the alley, and she talked the whole way through it about whether he had eaten today.

The bolt went back with a clack loud enough to carry.

"How long have you been able to do that?"

"A while."

"Agnes."

"I was wild in my younger days." She folded the case, put it back in the apron, stood, brushed off her knees. "That's the answer."

"That's not..." He stopped and restarted it flatter, because the first version was coming out too loud for an alley at four in the afternoon. "You carry them. The picks. In your apron, with the order slips, on a Tuesday, in a bakery."

"I do."

"Why?"

"Habit."

"Agnes, that's not..."

"Come inside, the coffee's going cold."

He let it go out loud, and filed it next to the corner table and the two cups of tea nobody drank, in a folder with her name on it that was getting thicker than he'd like.

---

The night of, they waited in the dark bakery with one light on in the back, and it turned out waiting was the part he was worst at.

Agnes handled it by working. She scaled flour for a morning she fully intended to have. Somewhere past ten she started talking, and it took Leo most of a minute to understand that she was answering a question he had never got around to asking.

"Seven years ago a man came in here with a shotgun and shot both of them with a shell full of salt."

Leo set the coffee down. "In here? In the bakery?"

"In the bakery. He apologized about the window afterward. Paid for it, too, which I have never once known what to do with." She leveled off the scoop. "I'd been seeing smudges for years by then. Things at the edge, nothing with a face on it. That night they had faces."

"Because of the salt?"

"Because of what it did to them." No drama in it at all. "They ran. It took me two nights to find them, in a warehouse off the freight yard on Ninth. I don't have a word for what they look like when they're hurt. It isn't blood. It's more like watching a photograph get lighter."

"What did you do?"

"I'd brought bread." She shrugged at the flour. "I don't know why. It's what I have."

He waited.

"Then the man came back. Rook. He'd followed me, of course he'd followed me, that's the entire thing he does." Her hands stopped. "He stood in the door of that warehouse with the gun up for about as long as I have ever had to keep talking without stopping."

"And?"

"And he put it down." She let out a breath she seemed to have been carrying for seven years. "He agreed to leave them alone, and he's kept it, which is more than I expected at the time. So I asked them to come here instead, because a warehouse is no place to be dead in."

"Why are you telling me this tonight?"

"Because in an hour you're going to pick up something that does worse."

She turned around and leaned back against the bench, and for the first time all evening she wasn't doing anything with her hands.

"There's one rule and this is it. It cannot make a sound while either of them is near it. Not a ring. Not a knock. It doesn't sort out what it's pointed at, it just goes, and it takes whatever else is in the room with it."

"How bad are we talking?"

Agnes listened toward the corner. Whatever came back took a while.

"Sable says it depends on how close. Across a room..." She stopped, and started again with her own words in it, which was how he could tell she had given up on the translation. "What she's describing is being hit by a truck. Standing next to it, worse than that. Holding it, she won't guess."

"But they'd still be here. After."

"They'd still be here. Laid out for a long time, no use to you or anybody for all of it, and neither of them can tell you how long, because the only people who'd know have been dead four hundred years." A pause. "That's the honest version. She wanted you to have the honest version."

"And Aldric?"

"Aldric says he's had worse." Her mouth went flat. "He hasn't. He's been telling jokes since six. That's what he does when he's scared."

Leo reached into the bag at his feet and put a folded rag on the bench. Then a roll of tape.

"We wrap the clapper before that thing moves an inch," he said. "Not once it's out of the case. Before it comes off the shelf. Anything in there swings once, and it only has to swing once, and I would rather find that out standing still in a dark room than halfway down a set of stairs."

Agnes looked at the rag. Then she went to the shelf by the proofing cabinet and came back with three flour-sack towels, worn soft, the ones she used on dough.

"Use these. Yours is too stiff, it'll slip."
