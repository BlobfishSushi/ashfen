# The Ledger

Leo had no case and had come anyway, which he had decided not to examine.

The bakery at four in the afternoon was the deadest hour of it. Agnes had the good tray out and was doing something at the far end of the counter that involved a bench scraper and a great deal of confidence, and she talked while she did it, mostly to herself, occasionally to him, and once to the oven.

The ghosts were at their table in the corner and had been going at each other for twenty minutes.

Leo got them the way he got everything from that corner, which was in pieces: the shape of a sentence, the temperature of it, every fourth or fifth word arriving intact and the rest sanded off somewhere before it reached him. Twenty minutes was long enough that he had stopped pretending to read his phone.

"What are they doing."

"Nope."

"Agnes."

"I'm not getting into it," Agnes said. She didn't look up. "I've been not getting into it since about 2019."

"Give me a topic. One word."

"No, because then I've picked a side, and then I'm in it, and then I'm the one who has to hear about it while I'm closing." She turned the dough. "You want a coffee? There's the end of the pot, it's not good."

"Yeah."

She got it and set it down and went back, and Leo drank the end of the pot, which was not good, and listened to two dead people argue in a corner about ten feet from him with about eighty percent of it missing.

"Okay," he said. "Ledger."

"Leo."

"That's a word I got clean, four times, so we can agree it's a ledger." He put the cup down. "And she said a number twice, and both times he answered before she finished it, which means he knows the number, which means it's a number he's heard a lot."

Agnes didn't say anything. She also didn't tell him to stop.

"Second floor," Leo said. "Got that one twice. So there's a building with at least two floors, and there's a ledger, and there's a number attached to the ledger that he has heard so many times he can finish it for her."

"You are so annoying."

"I've been told." He turned around in the chair to face the corner, which did not help, since it never helped. "And he keeps saying the same thing back. Same three or four syllables, same shape, every time she gets to the end. He's not arguing. He's got one line and he's running it out."

"He does have one line."

"Ha." Leo pointed at her without looking. "There it is. You're in it."

"I'm not in it."

"Four hundred years, Agnes. What is it, four hundred? They died in the sixteen hundreds, so four hundred years about a ledger on a second floor..." He stopped. "It's not there anymore, is it. The building."

Agnes wiped her hands.

"No," she said. "It's not."

"So neither of them can check."

"No."

"Seriously?" Leo sat back. "That's insane. That's an insane way to spend an afterlife. Four hundred years and neither of you can go look, and you're still..." He gestured at the corner, and the gesture got away from him a little. "You know there are people who'd kill for that kind of time. You know that, right? You two? Over there? People would kill."

Something happened in the corner. Agnes's mouth moved before she caught it.

"What," Leo said. "What did he say."

"He says," Agnes said, "and I'm going to clean this up, that he's tried to let it go, repeatedly, and that some people won't let a thing be finished."

"That's not what he said."

"It is not what he said, no."

Leo laughed, and it came out louder than the room, and he didn't mind. The oven ticked. Agnes went back to the bench scraper. He got maybe a minute of quiet before it started up again in the corner, the same rhythm, her long and him short, and Leo turned his cup around on the table and listened to a fifth of it.

She said the number. Aldric answered before she got there.

She said something else, longer this time, and there was a word in the middle of it that arrived whole, and it was *asked*.

Leo stopped turning the cup.

"It's not about the ledger," he said.

"Leo."

"She's not mad he moved it. She's mad he watched her look for it." He was talking to the corner now, not to Agnes. "How long. A week? She spent a week on it and you knew where it was, or you thought she was being ridiculous, so you let her go through the whole floor, and then at the end of it you told her, and you probably told her in a way you thought was funny."

He heard the last part of it out loud in his own voice and understood, roughly a syllable too late, what he had just done to a room he had walked into for no reason at all.

Nobody said anything for a while. Agnes had stopped working.

"Okay," Leo said. "You know what, I'm going to..."

"Sit down."

He sat down.

Agnes leaned on the counter with both hands and looked at the corner for a long moment, listening to something, her head tipped, the way she did when she was catching it in pieces herself and waiting for the rest.

"He says," she said, and then stopped, and started again. "He says it was nine days. Not a week."

"Nine days."

"And he says..." Agnes's face did something complicated. "'I did think it was funny. It was funny for about the first six.'"
